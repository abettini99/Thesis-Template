\section{Text}\label{sec:text}
%% =====================================================================

Body text is set in Times with indented paragraphs. Use \emph{emphasis} sparingly, \textbf{bold} even more so, and \textsc{small capitals} for the occasional acronym that should not shout. Quotation marks follow British style: \enquote{single quotes outside, with \enquote{double quotes} inside}. Numbers and units stay together on one line, as in a span of \qty{1.397}{\metre}, and references never start a line on their own, as in \cref{fig:single}.%

\subsection{Acronyms and symbols}

Acronyms and symbols are defined once and then appear in the nomenclature automatically; they are all defined in \file{_nomenclature.tex}. An acronym is written out the first time it is used: the \gls{bem} method is fast. After that, only the short form appears: \gls{bem} is used throughout. Symbols work in text and in maths alike: the dynamic pressure is $\frac{1}{2} \gls{rho} \gls{Uinf}^2$, with \gls{rho} the air density.

\section{Mathematics}\label{sec:maths}
%% =====================================================================

Inline maths such as $\Re = \rho U L / \mu$ uses the same Times letters as the text. Vectors and tensors are set in bold, as in the velocity $\vec{u}$ and the stress tensor $\tensor{\tau}$; writing them with \verb|\vec| and \verb|\tensor| lets you change that notation everywhere with a single change. For an incompressible flow, mass and momentum conservation read
\begin{align}
    \div \vec{u} &= 0, \label{eq:continuity} \\
    \sderiv{\vec{u}}{t} &= -\frac{1}{\rho} \grad p + \nu \nabla^2 \vec{u}, \label{eq:navier-stokes}
\end{align}
where the material derivative is
\begin{equation}
    \sderiv{\vec{u}}{t} = \pderiv{\vec{u}}{t} + \left( \vec{u} \cdot \grad \right) \vec{u}.
\end{equation}

A derivation over several lines numbers only its result:
\begin{align}
    \oint_{\Gamma} \vec{u} \cdot \diff{\vec{l}}
        &= \iint_{S} \left( \curl \vec{u} \right) \cdot \vec{n} \, \diff{S} \notag \\
        &= \iint_{S} \vec{\omega} \cdot \vec{n} \, \diff{S}. \label{eq:circulation}
\end{align}

Derivatives of any order, and their compact forms:
\begin{equation*}
    \deriv{y}{x}, \quad \deriv[2]{y}{x}, \quad \pderiv[2]{\phi}{x}, \quad \cpderiv{\phi}{x}, \quad \vec{A}\transpose \vec{B}, \quad \tensor{\sigma} \cddot \tensor{\varepsilon}.
\end{equation*}

Piecewise definitions, matrices and operators:
\begin{gather}
    C_L(\alpha) =
    \begin{cases}
        2\pi\alpha,             & |\alpha| < \alpha_\text{stall}, \\
        C_{L,\text{max}},       & \text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
    \qquad
    \tensor{R} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\
        \sin\theta & \cos\theta
    \end{bmatrix}, \\
    \hat{\vec{x}} = \argmin_{\vec{x}} \, \lVert \tensor{A} \vec{x} - \vec{b} \rVert_2^2 .
\end{gather}
Dimensionless numbers are upright: $\Re$, $\Ma$, $\Pr$, $\St$ and $\Fr$.

\subsection{Long equations}

On a narrow page, an equation that would fit on A4 often does not. Break it at an operator, and say where it continues. A single long expression is set with \texttt{multline}: the first line starts at the left, the last line ends at the right.
\begin{multline}
    \rho \left( \pderiv{u}{t} + u \pderiv{u}{x} + v \pderiv{u}{y} + w \pderiv{u}{z} \right) \\
    = -\pderiv{p}{x} + \mu \left( \pderiv[2]{u}{x} + \pderiv[2]{u}{y} + \pderiv[2]{u}{z} \right) + \rho g_x
    \label{eq:x-momentum}
\end{multline}
When the continuation should line up with an equals sign, \texttt{split} inside an equation gives one number for the whole:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        E &= \frac{1}{2} \rho \left( u^2 + v^2 + w^2 \right) + \rho e \\
          &\quad + \rho g z + \frac{1}{2} k \left( x - x_0 \right)^2 .
    \end{split}
    \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
Break before an operator ($+$, $-$, $=$), not after it, so the new line shows at once that it continues the previous one.

A weak form, as in a finite element method, consists of many inner products. Write them compactly: $\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}_D$ for the $L^2$ inner product over a domain $D$, with the domain as subscript, and time derivatives as $\cpderiv{\vec{u}^h}{t}$ rather than a fraction. Give the parts of the boundary short names, so the subscripts stay one level deep. A \texttt{formulation} environment sets the statement apart, as in \cref{form:burgers}; \texttt{subequations} numbers its equations (\ref{eq:fem-momentum})--(\ref{eq:fem-wall}) and lets the set be referred to as a whole, \cref{eq:fem}.

\begin{formulation}[Weak form of the viscous Burgers equation]\label{form:burgers}
Let the boundary consist of an inflow part and a wall, $\partial\Omega = \Gamma_\mathrm{in} \cup \Gamma_\mathrm{wall}$. Find $\vec{u}^h \in \mathcal{V}^h(\Omega)$ for $0 < t \leq T$, given an initial condition $\vec{u}_0$, such that for all $\vec{v} \in \mathcal{W}^h(\Omega)$, $\vec{\sigma} \in \mathcal{W}^h(\Gamma_\mathrm{in})$ and $\vec{\zeta} \in \mathcal{W}^h(\Gamma_\mathrm{wall})$:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:fem}
\begin{gather}
    \inner[\big]{\vec{v}}{\cpderiv{\vec{u}^h}{t}}_\Omega
    + \inner[\big]{\vec{v}}{\vec{u}^h \cdot \grad\vec{u}^h}_\Omega
    + \inner[\big]{\grad\vec{v}}{\nu \grad\vec{u}^h}_\Omega
    = \inner[\big]{\vec{v}}{\vec{f}}_\Omega ,                                 \label{eq:fem-momentum} \\
    \inner[\big]{\vec{\sigma}}{\vec{u}^h}_{\Gamma_\mathrm{in}}
    = \inner[\big]{\vec{\sigma}}{\vec{u}_\mathrm{in}}_{\Gamma_\mathrm{in}} ,       \label{eq:fem-inflow} \\
    \inner[\big]{\vec{\zeta}}{\vec{u}^h}_{\Gamma_\mathrm{wall}}
    = \inner[\big]{\vec{\zeta}}{\vec{u}_\mathrm{wall}}_{\Gamma_\mathrm{wall}} .    \label{eq:fem-wall}
\end{gather}
\end{subequations}
\end{formulation}

The command \verb|\inner| in \file{_options/commands.tex} writes the inner products. Displays give it a size, \verb|\inner[\big]|; in running text, leave out the size, $\inner{\vec{v}}{\vec{u}^h}_\Omega$, and use \verb|\inner*| for brackets that fit their contents. A coefficient such as $\nu$ goes inside the brackets; only a weight that defines the inner product itself, such as the radius $r$ in an axisymmetric problem, goes in the subscript, before the domain: $\inner{\vec{v}}{\vec{u}^h}_{r,\Omega}$.

When the same terms come back in later chapters, name them once, keeping the test function first as in the inner products:
\begin{equation*}
    a(\vec{v}, \vec{u}) \coloneqq \inner[\big]{\grad\vec{v}}{\nu \grad\vec{u}}_\Omega ,
    \qquad
    c(\vec{w}; \vec{v}, \vec{u}) \coloneqq \inner[\big]{\vec{v}}{\vec{w} \cdot \grad\vec{u}}_\Omega ,
\end{equation*}
so that \cref{eq:fem-momentum} becomes
\begin{equation*}
    \inner[\big]{\vec{v}}{\cpderiv{\vec{u}^h}{t}}_\Omega + c(\vec{u}^h; \vec{v}, \vec{u}^h) + a(\vec{v}, \vec{u}^h) = \inner[\big]{\vec{v}}{\vec{f}}_\Omega .
\end{equation*}
The diffusive form $a$ is bilinear; the convective form $c$ is linear in $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{u}$ but depends on the velocity $\vec{w}$ as well, which is why it is written with a semicolon.

\subsection{Definitions, lemmas and proofs}\label{sec:statements}

Definitions, lemmas, theorems and corollaries share one counter, so \cref{def:energy-norm} is followed by \cref{lem:coercivity}; a remark is unnumbered. All are set upright, which reads better than the usual italics when a statement is mostly maths.

\begin{definition}[Energy norm]\label{def:energy-norm}
For $\nu > 0$, the energy norm of $\vec{v} \in \mathcal{W}^h(\Omega)$ is $\lVert \vec{v} \rVert_a \coloneqq a(\vec{v}, \vec{v})^{1/2}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Coercivity]\label{lem:coercivity}
For constant $\nu > 0$, $a(\vec{v}, \vec{v}) = \nu \lVert \grad\vec{v} \rVert_\Omega^2 \geq 0$ for all $\vec{v} \in \mathcal{W}^h(\Omega)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
With $\nu$ constant, it can be taken out of the inner product:
\begin{equation*}
    a(\vec{v}, \vec{v}) = \inner[\big]{\grad\vec{v}}{\nu \grad\vec{v}}_\Omega = \nu \inner[\big]{\grad\vec{v}}{\grad\vec{v}}_\Omega = \nu \lVert \grad\vec{v} \rVert_\Omega^2 \geq 0 . \qedhere
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
For $\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ on the boundary, $c(\vec{w}; \vec{v}, \vec{v}) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \inner{\grad\cdot\vec{w}}{\lvert\vec{v}\rvert^2}_\Omega$, which vanishes only if $\vec{w}$ is divergence free. The velocity of the Burgers equation is not divergence free, so stability analyses use the skew-symmetric form
\begin{equation*}
    \tilde{c}(\vec{w}; \vec{v}, \vec{u}) \coloneqq c(\vec{w}; \vec{v}, \vec{u}) + \tfrac{1}{2} \inner[\big]{\vec{v}}{(\grad\cdot\vec{w})\, \vec{u}}_\Omega ,
\end{equation*}
for which $\tilde{c}(\vec{w}; \vec{v}, \vec{v}) = 0$ whenever $\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ on the boundary.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Algorithms}\label{sec:algorithms}

Algorithms are floats like figures, with the caption on top (\cref{alg:picard}). Number the lines and refer to them as well: the linear system is solved in line~\ref{alg:picard-solve}.

\begin{algorithm}[tbp]
    \caption{Picard iteration for one time step of \cref{form:burgers}.}
    \label{alg:picard}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require solution $\vec{u}^n$, time step $\Delta t$, tolerance $\varepsilon$
        \Ensure solution $\vec{u}^{n+1}$
        \State $\vec{w} \gets \vec{u}^n$ \Comment{initial guess}
        \Repeat
            \State assemble $\mathsf{A}(\vec{w})$ and $\mathsf{b}$ from $c(\vec{w}; \cdot, \cdot)$ and $a$
            \State solve $\mathsf{A}(\vec{w})\, \vec{u}^\ast = \mathsf{b}$ \label{alg:picard-solve}
            \State $\delta \gets \lVert \vec{u}^\ast - \vec{w} \rVert_\Omega$
            \State $\vec{w} \gets \vec{u}^\ast$
        \Until{$\delta < \varepsilon$}
        \State \Return $\vec{u}^{n+1} \gets \vec{w}$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Computing the lift coefficient from thin-aerofoil theory.}, label={lst:example}]
import numpy as np

def lift_coefficient(alpha_deg):
    # Thin-aerofoil theory: C_L = 2 pi alpha
    alpha = np.radians(alpha_deg)
    return 2 * np.pi * alpha

for alpha in range(0, 12, 2):
    print(f"alpha = {alpha:2d} deg, C_L = {lift_coefficient(alpha):.3f}")
\end{lstlisting}
